# Bone Health During Menopause

## Why bone health changes at menopause
Adequate calcium intake becomes especially important after menopause because declining estrogen increases bone loss and calcium absorption can decline with age.
Estrogen plays an important role in maintaining bone density. As estrogen levels decline during menopause, bone breakdown can outpace bone formation, leading to faster bone loss than at earlier life stages. This raises the risk of osteoporosis and fractures over time, making this a critical period to actively support bone health through diet and lifestyle.

## Calcium needs increase
Calcium requirements rise during and after menopause compared to earlier reproductive years, since the body needs more dietary calcium to help offset the faster rate of bone loss. Good sources include:
- Dairy: milk, curd, paneer, cheese
- Ragi (finger millet), which is unusually high in calcium for a grain
- Sesame seeds (til) and til-based sweets like til laddoo
- Green leafy vegetables like methi and drumstick leaves
- Fortified foods where available

## Vitamin D is essential alongside calcium
Calcium cannot be properly absorbed and used by bones without adequate vitamin D. Vitamin D needs also rise during this life stage. Since very few foods naturally contain much vitamin D, sunlight exposure and, where appropriate, fortified foods become especially important (see the separate vitamin D document for specific guidance).

## Weight-bearing exercise
Physical activity that puts stress on bones, such as walking, climbing stairs, dancing, or light resistance training, signals the body to maintain or build bone density. This is considered just as important as diet for bone health at this stage. Even 30 minutes of brisk walking most days can meaningfully support bone strength when combined with adequate calcium and vitamin D.

## Other supportive factors
- Adequate protein intake supports the bone matrix, not just bone mineral content
- Limiting excessive caffeine and salt can help reduce calcium loss through urine
- Avoiding smoking and limiting alcohol are both associated with better bone density outcomes

## What this does not replace
Diet and exercise support bone health but cannot fully prevent osteoporosis in everyone, especially where genetics, prior fractures, or other risk factors are involved. Bone density testing (DEXA scan) and, where needed, medical treatment are decisions to make with a doctor. Anyone with joint pain, height loss, or a fracture from a minor fall should seek medical evaluation rather than relying on diet alone.
