# Food and Medication Interactions

## Why this matters
Certain common foods and nutrients can interact with medications in ways that reduce the medication's effectiveness or, less commonly, increase side effects. Being aware of these interactions helps people time their meals and medications sensibly, though any specific concern should be raised with a doctor or pharmacist rather than resolved through general information alone.

## Calcium and thyroid medication
Calcium (from dairy, supplements, or calcium-rich foods) can significantly reduce the absorption of thyroid hormone replacement medication (such as levothyroxine) if taken too close together. The general guidance is to separate calcium-rich food or supplements from thyroid medication by at least **4 hours**, and to take thyroid medication on an empty stomach as typically directed by a doctor. This is one of the most well-documented food-drug interactions relevant to women's health, since thyroid conditions and calcium needs (especially post-menopause) often overlap.

## Iron and tea/coffee
As covered in the iron-related documents, tannins in tea and coffee bind to iron and reduce its absorption. This applies to both dietary iron and iron supplements. If someone is taking an iron supplement, it should generally be taken with a vitamin C source and away from tea or coffee, based on their doctor's specific instructions.

## Iron and calcium
Iron and calcium can compete for absorption when taken together in large amounts. If someone is taking both an iron supplement and a calcium supplement, doctors often recommend spacing them a few hours apart rather than taking them at the same time.

## Vitamin K and blood thinners
Leafy green vegetables are high in vitamin K, which plays a role in blood clotting. People on blood-thinning medication (such as warfarin) are often advised to keep their vitamin K intake consistent day to day, rather than eliminating leafy greens, since sudden large changes in intake (in either direction) can affect how the medication works. This should always be managed under a doctor's guidance, not through self-directed dietary changes.

## Grapefruit and certain medications
Grapefruit and grapefruit juice can interfere with how the body processes several types of medications, including some used for blood pressure and cholesterol. This is less directly related to this app's core nutrients but is a commonly asked-about interaction worth being aware of.

## General guidance
Anyone taking regular medication should mention their diet and any supplement use to their doctor or pharmacist, since interactions can be specific to the exact medication, dose, and individual health situation. This document provides general awareness, not a complete or personalized safety check.

## Important note
This information is not a substitute for professional medical or pharmacist advice. Anyone with questions about their specific medication and diet should consult their doctor or pharmacist directly rather than relying on general guidance.
